कर्म Karma

Not a vending machine. Not fate. Not reward and punishment. Something far more interesting — and far more demanding.

Before we begin

What does karma actually mean?

Click the definition you think is closest. Then we'll show you what every Hindu philosophical tradition actually says.

"What goes around comes around."
A cosmic balancing act — good in, good out
✗ Not in any classical Hindu text
This is the Western popular version — karma as a kind of moral vending machine. It appears nowhere in the Vedas, Upaniṣads, or Bhagavad-gītā in this form. The idea that the universe automatically rewards and punishes is closer to a folk belief than a philosophical doctrine.
"Fate — what was always meant to be."
Your destiny, fixed by past lives
✗ The opposite of what karma means
Karma is emphatically not fate. The entire practical force of the doctrine is that you can change your trajectory through intentional action right now. A fatalistic reading of karma would make the Bhagavad-gītā's central argument — act rightly, act now — incoherent.
"Good deeds bring good results. Bad deeds bring suffering."
A moral law of cause and effect
≈ Closer — but it misses the point
This captures part of the Mīmāṃsā reading (ritual action brings correct results). But the Bhagavad-gītā — the most influential karma text — specifically argues that attachment to results is the problem. The question isn't what the action produces; it's what the action does to the person who performs it.
"Every intentional action leaves an impression that shapes who you become."
Action as a force that moulds the agent
✓ This is the original philosophical claim
Karma (from the Sanskrit root kṛ, "to do") refers to action and its residue. Every intentional act leaves a saṃskāra — an impression in the mind. Saṃskāras accumulate into vāsanās, deep tendencies that shape future choices. You don't just do karma; karma does you.
The word karma simply means action. Its power as a philosophical concept comes from what action leaves behind — not in the world, but in the person who acts. This is what the next section explores.

The mechanism

How karma actually works

Scroll through the steps. The diagram builds as you go.

Step 01
The word means action

Karma comes from the Sanskrit root kṛ — to do, to make, to act. In the oldest Vedic texts, it referred specifically to ritual action: the correct performance of a yajña (fire sacrifice). The priest's karma was his precise enactment of the rite. The philosophical meaning grew from this: all intentional action is, in this sense, ritual — consequential, shaping the world and the actor alike.

कर्म (kṛ — to do)
Step 02
Every action leaves a saṃskāra

The term saṃskāra literally means "putting together perfectly" — a refinement, an impression. Every intentional act deposits a saṃskāra in the mind — a trace, a groove. Like water carving a channel, repeated actions deepen those channels. The mind becomes predisposed. This is why practice — of virtue, of concentration, of devotion — works: you are literally reshaping the substrate of your future choices.

संस्कार — impression, refinement
Step 03
Saṃskāras become vāsanās

Vāsanā means "perfume" — the scent that clings after the flower is gone. Accumulated saṃskāras crystallise into vāsanās: deep-seated tendencies that operate below the level of conscious choice. They generate desires, fears, and compulsions. You don't choose to crave what you crave; your vāsanās do. This is why liberation in most Hindu traditions requires addressing not just actions, but the deepest conditioning of the mind.

वासना — subtle tendency, perfume
Step 04
Vāsanās generate future action

Vāsanās surface as desires, which generate further actions, which lay down new saṃskāras — and the cycle perpetuates itself. This is why simply willing yourself to behave differently rarely works: you are trying to change the surface while the deep currents run unchanged. The tradition's insight is radical: you cannot think your way out of karma. You have to act your way out — and act differently, from a different motivation.

वासना → कर्म → संस्कार
Step 05
This is saṃsāra — not punishment, but physics

The cycle — action, impression, tendency, action — drives saṃsāra, the ongoing round of existence. The tradition does not call this evil. It calls it the nature of conditioned existence. The way out is not to stop acting (the Bhagavad-gītā explicitly rules this out) but to act without ego-attachment — niṣkāma karma, action free of craving for its fruits. This is the Gītā's central argument. Scroll on to read the exact verse.

निष्काम कर्म — desireless action
Technique: word-level annotation · guided tour · hover tooltip
Bhagavad-gītā 2.47

The most important sentence in karma philosophy

Kṛṣṇa has one answer to Arjuna's paralysis. It is six words in Sanskrit. Hover over each word — or take the guided tour.

कर्मण्येवाधिकारस्ते मा फलेषु कदाचन । मा कर्मफलहेतुर्भूर्मा ते सङ्गोऽस्त्वकर्मणि ॥
karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana | mā karmaphalahetur bhūr mā te saṅgo 'stv akarmaṇi ||
Bhagavad-gītā 2.47 — Kṛṣṇa to Arjuna
karmaṇi
in action
karmaṇi — locative case of karma. "In the field of action." The verse locates you firmly in the domain of doing — inaction is not offered as an option.
eva
only / alone
eva — an emphatic particle. "In action alone." This is not a suggestion. Kṛṣṇa is ruling out any alternative. Action is your field, full stop.
adhikāras
right / authority
adhikāras — right, entitlement, authority, jurisdiction. You have jurisdiction over your actions. You do not have jurisdiction over their outcomes.
te
your
te — yours, belonging to you. This is personal, direct. Kṛṣṇa is speaking to Arjuna specifically — and, by extension, to the reader specifically.
mā phaleṣu
not in fruits
mā phaleṣu — "not in the fruits." The fruits (phala) are the results — victory, wealth, reputation, success. These are categorically not your domain. You do not control them.
kadācana
ever / at any time
kadācana — ever, at any time, under any circumstances. This is absolute. Not "mostly avoid attachment to results" — never, at any time, attach your identity to outcomes.
mā karmaphala-hetur bhūḥ
don't be the cause of results
mā karmaphala-hetur bhūḥ — "do not let the fruits of action be your motive." Don't perform actions because of what they will get you. This is the core of niṣkāma karma — action without desire for its results.
mā te saṅgo 'stv akarmaṇi
no attachment to inaction
mā te saṅgo 'stv akarmaṇi — "let there be no attachment to inaction." The verse closes the escape route. You cannot retreat into passivity. Act — but act without clinging to the outcome.
Full translation
"You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions. Never consider yourself the cause of the results of your activities, and never be attached to not doing your duty."

Three readings

How different traditions interpret karma

The word is shared. The meaning is not. Select a school to see how its framework changes everything.

Core claim
Karma is ritual performance
For Mīmāṃsā, karma means the Vedic injunction — the command to perform a ritual correctly. Dharma is defined as that which is enjoined by the Veda. The fruits of action (svarga, heaven) are guaranteed if the ritual is performed precisely. This is the most "literal" reading: karma as correct action producing its mandated result.
Emphasis on correct performanceVery high
Emphasis on inner motivationVery low
Key implication
What matters is what you do, not why
Mīmāṃsā holds the Vedas as eternally authorless — and the rituals they prescribe are the mechanism by which the universe maintains itself. The agent's inner state is largely irrelevant; the act's correctness is everything. This position is directly challenged by the Bhagavad-gītā, which puts motivation at the centre.
A Level relevance: Mīmāṃsā's framework underlies the debate between "act ethics" and "motivational ethics" in Hindu thought — and maps onto similar debates in Western moral philosophy.
Core claim
Karma is bondage — niṣkāma karma is the door
For Advaita, the ultimate reality is Brahman — pure, non-dual consciousness. The individual self (jīva) appears separate only because of avidyā (ignorance). Karma is the mechanism of that ignorance: action driven by ego-attachment generates saṃskāras that reinforce the illusion of a separate self. The solution is jñāna — direct knowledge of the self's non-difference from Brahman.
Karma as the problemHigh
Jñāna (knowledge) as solutionSupreme
Key implication
You transcend karma by realising there is no separate "you"
Śaṅkara reads the Bhagavad-gītā as a provisional teaching: karma yoga is appropriate for those not yet ready for jñāna. The truly liberated act freely — because for them, there is no agent, no action, and no fruit. "The knower of Brahman does not act, because in Brahman there is no becoming."
A Level relevance: This position is central to debates about free will, agency, and moral responsibility in Hindu ethics.
Core claim
Karma is transcended by bhakti — devotional action
For Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism, both karma (duty-action) and jñāna (knowledge-path) are limited. Bhakti — pure, unmotivated devotion to Kṛṣṇa — is not subject to the law of karma at all. The devotee acts entirely for Kṛṣṇa's pleasure, with no self-interest. This action generates no karmic residue because the ego-agent has been replaced by the devotee-servant.
Bhakti as transcending karmaSupreme
Relationship with GodCentral
Key implication
Love makes karma irrelevant
Jīva Gosvāmī argues that the Bhagavad-gītā's karma yoga is not the destination but the preparation — purifying the mind for bhakti. Once bhakti is established, the practitioner is "beyond the Vedas" (vedātīta). The Bhāgavata Purāṇa puts it simply: "One who acts devotionally for Viṣṇu is released from all karma."
A Level relevance: This maps onto debates about whether ethics (doing right) or religion (loving God) is the deeper category — a question that appears across Hindu, Christian, and Islamic ethics.
