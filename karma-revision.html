Section 01
What is Karma?

The Sanskrit word karma derives from the root kṛ — to do, to act, to make. In its simplest sense, karma means action. In its philosophical sense, it means action and the residue action leaves in the agent who performs it.

Karma is not fate. It is not a cosmic reward-and-punishment system. It is not "what goes around comes around." These are popular Western readings that appear in no classical Hindu text. The tradition's claim is more precise — and more demanding: every intentional action shapes the person who performs it, and the accumulated shape of a person determines their future choices and circumstances.

karma
Action; intentional deed; from the root kṛ — to do
saṃskāra
Mental impression left by action; a "refining" trace in the mind
vāsanā
Deep tendency accumulated from saṃskāras; literally "perfume"
phala
Fruit / result of action — the Gītā says this is not your domain
The etymology matters. Always define karma as "action" before developing its philosophical significance. Examiners reward precise starting definitions.
Karma does NOT mean "fate" or "destiny." Confusing karma with determinism is one of the most common errors — karma is specifically about the freedom and responsibility to act rightly, not passively accept what comes.
Section 02
The Karma Mechanism: Action → Impression → Tendency → Action

The karma cycle operates through three stages:

1. Action (karma) — Every intentional act, word, or thought leaves a trace. Not involuntary acts (reflex, accident) — but chosen, motivated actions.

2. Saṃskāra (impression) — Each intentional act deposits an impression in the mind-stuff (citta). Like a groove worn by water, repeated actions deepen these grooves. The mind becomes predisposed.

3. Vāsanā (tendency) — Accumulated saṃskāras crystallise into vāsanās: deep, below-conscious tendencies that generate future desires, fears, and motivations — which generate further actions.

"The mind is the cause of bondage and the cause of liberation." — Maitri Upaniṣad 6.34 Key implication: liberation requires transforming the mind at the level of vāsanās, not merely changing outward behaviour
Being able to trace the chain Action → Saṃskāra → Vāsanā → Action demonstrates high-level AO1 knowledge. It's the mechanism behind saṃsāra — essential for explaining why liberation is difficult.
Section 03
Karma and Saṃsāra: The Cycle of Rebirth

Saṃsāra (literally "flowing through") is the ongoing cycle of birth, death, and rebirth. Karma is its engine. At death, the accumulated karma of a life shapes the conditions of the next — not as punishment, but as consequence. The tradition describes this in terms of punarjanman (rebirth) driven by unspent karma and unresolved vāsanās.

The form of rebirth — human, animal, divine, infernal — depends on the quality and quantity of karmic residue. Traditions vary: some describe a literal chain of births; others (especially Advaita) treat saṃsāra as ultimately an appearance driven by ignorance (avidyā).

"As a person puts on new garments, giving up old ones, similarly, the soul accepts new material bodies, giving up the old and useless ones." — Bhagavad-gītā 2.22 The most commonly cited verse for rebirth — context is Kṛṣṇa explaining why death of the body is not death of the ātman
saṃsāra
The cycle of birth, death, and rebirth; literally "flowing together"
punarjanman
Rebirth; the continuation of the ātman in a new body shaped by karma
Link karma directly to saṃsāra — they are almost always examined together. Show the mechanism: unspent karma → conditions of next birth → further action → more karma → cycle perpetuates.
Section 04
Karma and Mokṣa: The Way Out

Mokṣa (liberation, release) is freedom from the karma-saṃsāra cycle. What it means varies by tradition:

Advaita Vedānta: Mokṣa is the realisation that Ātman = Brahman and that saṃsāra was never ultimately real. Karma dissolves when the false ego (the agent who accumulates it) is seen through. Jīvanmukti — liberation while living — is possible.

Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism: Mokṣa is eternal relationship with Kṛṣṇa in a transcendent realm. Bhakti (devotional love) does not produce karma at all — it transcends the karma system entirely. The devotee acts for Kṛṣṇa's pleasure, leaving no karmic residue.

Mīmāṃsā: Correct ritual action does not bind but liberates through its prescribed fruits — the tradition is less focused on mokṣa and more on the correctness of action itself.

"When a wise person sees no other agent than the Self, and when he realises the unity of the Self, there is no more karma for him." — Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.4.23 Advaita reading: karma ceases when the illusion of separate agency dissolves
Mokṣa is the evaluative hinge of most karma questions. A strong AO2 argument will compare different traditions' definitions of what liberation from karma actually means.
Section 05
Karma and Dharma: Action and Right Order

Dharma (right order, duty, law) and karma are inseparable: karma is the mechanism; dharma is the standard by which action is judged. Actions in accord with dharma tend toward liberation; actions against dharma reinforce unfavourable karma.

The Bhagavad-gītā's central crisis is precisely a conflict of dharmas: Arjuna's warrior-dharma (kṣatriya dharma) commands him to fight; his relational dharma as a son and student commands him not to. Kṛṣṇa's answer reframes the question: act according to your svadharma (own nature and duty), without ego-attachment to the outcome.

"Better is one's own dharma, though imperfectly performed, than the dharma of another well performed." — Bhagavad-gītā 3.35 svadharma — the dharma specific to your nature and social position
The karma-dharma link is essential for ethics questions. Dharma provides the content (what to do); karma provides the mechanism (how action binds or liberates). Don't treat them as synonyms.
Section 06 · Key Primary Text
Bhagavad-gītā 2.47 — The Central Karma Verse
"You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions. Never consider yourself the cause of the results of your activities, and never be attached to not doing your duty." Bhagavad-gītā 2.47 — Kṛṣṇa to Arjuna, on the battlefield of Kurukṣetra
karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana |
mā karmaphalahetur bhūr mā te saṅgo 'stv akarmaṇi ||

Line by line:

karmaṇy evādhikāras te — Your authority/jurisdiction is in action alone. You are not authorised over the fruits.

mā phaleṣu kadācana — Never, at any time, in the fruits. This is absolute.

mā karmaphalahetur bhūḥ — Do not let desire for results be your motive for acting.

mā te saṅgo 'stv akarmaṇi — Do not be attached to inaction either. Retreat is not an option.

This verse must be memorised in translation and ideally in transliteration. It appears in almost every karma question at every board level. Ability to break it down word by word demonstrates top-band AO1.
Do not cite this verse as simply "do your duty." The argument is more specific: perform action without attachment to outcomes. The Gītā is arguing against both desire-driven action AND against inaction.
Section 07
Niṣkāma Karma: Desireless Action

Niṣkāma karma (from niṣkāma — without desire) is the Bhagavad-gītā's solution to the karma problem. Act, but act without ego-attachment to the outcome. Perform your duty because it is your duty — not because of what you will gain or lose by it.

This is demanding because it does not mean acting without effort or care — it means acting with full engagement while remaining psychologically detached from the result. Kṛṣṇa's model is the expert craftsman who works with complete attention but does not define themselves by whether the project succeeds.

The philosophical implication: niṣkāma karma does not accumulate karmic residue because there is no ego-agent investing in the outcome. The vāsanā system is broken at its source — the desire that generates impressions.

"Therefore, without attachment, always perform the action that should be done — for by performing action without attachment, one attains the Supreme." — Bhagavad-gītā 3.19 tasmād asaktaḥ satataṃ kāryaṃ karma samācara
Niṣkāma karma is specifically OCR's most-examined karma concept. Know the argument: (1) you cannot avoid action; (2) action with ego-desire creates karma; (3) therefore act without desire for results.
Section 08
Three Interpretations: How Traditions Read Karma Differently
Mīmāṃsā
Karma = ritual action. Correct performance of the Vedic injunction produces its prescribed result. Inner motivation is less relevant than outward correctness.
"The Vedic injunction alone has the force of dharma." — Jaimini, Mīmāṃsā Sūtras 1.1.2
Advaita Vedānta
Karma is bondage arising from avidyā (ignorance). Jñāna (direct knowledge of Ātman = Brahman) dissolves karma at its root. Niṣkāma karma is preparatory, not final.
"The knower of Brahman becomes Brahman." — Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 3.2.9
Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism
Bhakti (devoted love for Kṛṣṇa) transcends karma entirely. The devotee acts only for Kṛṣṇa's pleasure — this generates no karmic residue. Karma yoga is preparation for bhakti.
"One who acts devotionally for Viṣṇu is freed from all karma." — Bhāgavata Purāṇa 3.33.6
When comparing traditions, always show what they agree on (karma binds; liberation is possible) before showing what they disagree on (the mechanism and route of liberation). Examiners reward structured comparison, not just listing differences.
Section 09 · AO2
Evaluation: Strengths and Weaknesses of Karma as a Moral Framework
Strengths
Moral responsibility — karma locates ethical consequences within the agent, not an external judge. It encourages personal accountability.
Weaknesses
Social quietism — karma can be used to justify existing inequalities (caste, poverty) as the "deserved" result of past lives.
Strengths
Long-term ethics — the karmic framework takes a broader view of moral time than single-life consequentialism. Actions have consequences beyond the visible horizon.
Weaknesses
Empirical unverifiability — the claim that actions in this life affect the next is not empirically testable. Critics (e.g. B.R. Ambedkar) rejected karma as ideologically convenient for upper castes.
Strengths
Motivation for reform — the doctrine of saṃskāra implies that character is changeable. You are not fixed; practice can reshape the deepest tendencies. Psychologically sophisticated.
Weaknesses
Determinism tension — if future lives are shaped by past karma, how much freedom does the agent actually have? The tradition responds, but the tension is real and worth exploring.
Scholar voices for AO2
Wendy Doniger — argues karma functions more as a narrative and meaning-making device than a verifiable metaphysical claim. B.R. Ambedkar — viewed karma theology as a tool of Brahminical oppression; converted to Buddhism in 1956 as an explicit rejection of the doctrine. Alasdair MacIntyre — comparative point: karma shares the virtue-ethics emphasis on character over isolated acts.
Top-band AO2 requires evaluating both the internal consistency of the argument AND its external plausibility. Show you can argue from multiple positions — not just "karma is good because..." or "karma is wrong because..."
Section 10
Connections: How Karma Links to Other Specification Topics
Karma → Dharma
Dharma determines what right action looks like; karma is the mechanism by which acting well or badly shapes the agent
Karma → Mokṣa
Liberation is defined, in all schools, as freedom from the karma-saṃsāra cycle — the goal shapes how karma is understood
Karma → Ahiṃsā
Non-violence is partly justified through karma: violence creates violent saṃskāras and harmful karma for the agent
Karma → Varṇa/Caste
Caste has been theologically justified as the result of karma from previous lives — one of the doctrine's most contested applications
Karma → Reform
Reform movements (Ārya Samāj, Ambedkar) specifically challenged karma theology as a barrier to social equality
Karma → Gender
Traditional texts attributed birth as a woman to past karma — contested by contemporary Hindu feminists
Karma rarely appears as an isolated topic in exam questions. It is almost always linked to at least one other concept — dharma, mokṣa, or social ethics. Practise connecting these in your revision essays.
