What is Hinduism?

Not one thing —
a civilisation of religions

The word arrives first in Persian and then in Arabic, used to describe the people living beyond the Sindhu — the Indus river. It was, at the start, a geographical term, not a religious one. The people it described had no single name for themselves, no single founder, no single creed. What they shared was something stranger and more interesting: a civilisation.

Today, over a billion people identify as Hindu, making it the world's third-largest religion. But calling it a religion understates it. Hinduism encompasses dozens of philosophical schools, hundreds of sects, thousands of deities, and an entire civilisation of art, music, mathematics, and law — all without a pope, a single sacred text, or a founding moment. Where Christianity has the Sermon on the Mount and Islam has the first revelation to Muḥammad, Hinduism has no such origin point. It simply… arose.

"Ekam sad viprā bahudhā vadanti — Reality is one; the wise speak of it in many ways."
Ṛg Veda 1.164.46 — written c. 1500–1200 BCE
Why it resists definition — and why that's the point

Every religion contains diversity within it; Hinduism is unusual in naming and celebrating that diversity rather than suppressing it. The line above, written perhaps 3,500 years ago, functions as a kind of constitutional principle: there is one truth, and many legitimate ways of approaching it. The result is not one religion but a conversation — sometimes harmonious, sometimes fierce — that has been going on for millennia without resolution. And without needing resolution.

This is not vagueness. It is a specific philosophical position: that ultimate reality is too large to be captured by any single description, and that the human project is to approach it from the angle your nature and your circumstances allow. A scholar approaches through inquiry. A devotee through love. A contemplative through stillness. A person of action through service. The tradition holds all four as equally valid paths to the same destination.

What the eight concepts on the map are for

The concepts on the mandala above are the vocabulary of this conversation. They are not beliefs you must hold to be Hindu — Hinduism has no creed. They are the ideas the conversation keeps returning to: the nature of reality (Brahman), the nature of the self (Ātman), the veil between them (Māyā), how to act rightly (Dharma), the mechanism of cause and consequence (Karma), the cycle of existence (Saṃsāra), the paths of practice (Yoga), and the goal of liberation (Mokṣa). Start anywhere. Follow what resonates. The tradition is old enough, and wide enough, to hold your question.
