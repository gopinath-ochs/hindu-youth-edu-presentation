The six darśanas are often called the "orthodox schools" of Hindu philosophy. But that label misses something. These weren't dogmas handed down from on high — they were competing research programs, each starting from the Vedas and arriving somewhere radically different. Sāṃkhya says consciousness and matter are two eternal principles. Advaita says they're not even real distinctions. Both cite the same texts.

The Six Darśanas · Essay 03 of 06
Nyāya: the school that built logic itself
How ancient Indian philosophers created a rigorous epistemology — and why it still matters for anyone who wants to think clearly.

Most people encounter Indian philosophy through its mystical side — the Upanishads, meditation, the nature of consciousness. Nyāya comes from the other direction. It asks: before we talk about reality, can we agree on what counts as good reasoning?

"The Naiyāyikas weren't interested in transcendence. They wanted a philosophy you could argue with."

The Nyāya school developed a system of pramāṇas — valid sources of knowledge. Four of them: perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. Aristotle was working on similar problems in Greece at roughly the same time. Neither knew the other existed.
